Abstract

NFL field goal outcomes are observed under a selective decision process: coaches choose when a kick is attempted, and that selection mechanism can bias kicker evaluation and probability calibration. This study compares three model classes on 2015-2024 NFL kickable states: a baseline success model trained on observed attempts (M1), an inverse probability weighted variant using attempt propensity scores (M2), and an augmented pseudo-label robustness variant (M3). The attempt propensity model is strong across seasons (AUC approximately 0.94-0.96), indicating that coaching behavior is predictable from game state. In-sample, M2 improves fit relative to M1 (Brier 0.0985 vs 0.1005), but out-of-sample predictive performance remains strongest for M1 (global test Brier 0.0975 and AUC 0.7719, versus M2 Brier 0.1001 and AUC 0.7596). M3 shifts long-range calibration in the expected direction (50+ mean probability approximately 0.646 versus baseline approximately 0.676), but does not improve predictive performance and is treated as exploratory. The practical recommendation is to keep M1 as the predictive benchmark, use M2 as the primary correction attempt for selection bias diagnostics, and position M3 as a secondary robustness lens for tail-behavior interpretation.

1. Introduction

1.1 Motivation: Selection Bias in Kicker Evaluation

Field goal modeling is not only a shot-making problem. It is also a selection problem. The observed dataset contains kicks that coaches chose to attempt, while many kickable fourth-down situations are censored by strategic alternatives such as punts or offensive plays. If this selection is ignored, model-based kicker evaluation can inherit survivorship bias: probabilities appear better calibrated than they are in the full decision space, and long-distance performance can be overstated.

This project investigates whether explicit modeling of the attempt decision improves estimation of field goal success probabilities and, ultimately, kicker evaluation. The core tension is practical and methodological: correcting selection bias can improve descriptive fit in difficult states while still failing to improve out-of-sample prediction.

1.2 Project Objective and Research Questions

The primary objective is to quantify how much predictive and evaluative behavior changes when attempt selection is modeled explicitly.

Research questions:

  1. Does inverse probability weighting of observed attempts (M2) improve predictive accuracy relative to an unweighted baseline mixed model (M1)?
  2. Do pseudo-labeled non-attempt augmentations (M3) improve calibration in long-distance tails, and at what predictive cost?
  3. Is the coaching attempt mechanism sufficiently structured to support a propensity-based correction framework?

Working hypotheses:

  1. The attempt propensity model will be strongly predictive of observed kick decisions.
  2. M2 will improve in-sample fit by reweighting under-observed difficult attempts.
  3. M1 will remain the strongest out-of-sample predictive model.
  4. M3 will provide directional calibration insight in the tail, but not predictive gains.

1.3 Modeling Frame

The manuscript uses a pi-first selection framework. Let \(A_i\) denote whether a kick is attempted and \(Y_i\) denote kick success when attempted. The decision process is modeled by \(\hat{\pi}(X_i)=P(A_i=1\mid X_i)\) over kickable game states. Success models are then compared under three training strategies:

  1. M1 baseline mixed model on observed attempts.
  2. M2 weighted mixed model using stabilized and clipped inverse propensity weights.
  3. M3 exploratory augmented training with conservative pseudo-label influence.

This structure separates predictive benchmarking from bias-correction diagnostics.

1.4 Preview of Findings

Current evidence from the project artifacts supports the following high-level summary:

  1. Attempt propensity is stable and highly predictive across seasons.
  2. M2 improves in-sample fit but does not outperform M1 on held-out prediction.
  3. M3 alters long-distance calibration behavior but degrades predictive metrics versus M1, and therefore remains exploratory.

These findings motivate a two-layer interpretation: M1 as predictive benchmark, M2 as main selection-bias correction attempt, and M3 as secondary robustness analysis.
